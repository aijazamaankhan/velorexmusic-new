Vinyl is durable when it is looked after and surprisingly fragile when it is not. In most of India the two things that damage record collections are the same two things that define the weather: heat and humidity. Heat warps discs. Damp grows mould on sleeves and, eventually, on the records themselves. This guide covers how to store a collection so it plays well for decades — whether you own five records or five hundred.

Always store records upright

Records should stand vertically, like books on a shelf, snug enough that they do not lean but not so tight that pulling one out is a struggle. Stacking records flat, one on top of another, puts the weight of the whole pile on the bottom discs; over time that causes ring wear on the sleeves and can warp the records underneath.

Leaning is the other common mistake. A half-empty shelf where records slump at an angle puts uneven pressure on each disc. Use a divider or a bookend to keep them straight.

Keep them away from heat

Vinyl softens with heat, and a record that softens while bent will set in that shape. The most common causes of warped records at home are simple ones:

  • a shelf that gets direct sunlight for part of the day, even through a window;
  • a wall shared with a kitchen, or a spot above or beside an appliance that runs hot;
  • the back seat or boot of a car — never leave records in a parked car, especially in summer;
  • an outside wall that heats up through the afternoon, and a room with no ventilation.

An inner room, away from windows, that stays at a steady temperature through the day is the best place for a collection.

Keep humidity steady, and plan for the monsoon

High humidity does not warp records, but it feeds mould and mildew on cardboard sleeves and paper inner sleeves — the tell-tale signs are a musty smell, grey-white spots and wavy board. Once mould reaches the disc it can leave marks in the grooves that are audible.

  • Avoid extremes and sudden swings. Archives that care for sound recordings aim for cool, dry and — above all — stable conditions. A home cannot match an archive, but you can avoid the worst places: bathrooms, basements, and walls that get damp in the rains.
  • Let air move. A closed cupboard that is never opened traps damp during the monsoon. Open it regularly, or leave a gap for air to circulate.
  • Check the collection after the rains. Pull out a few records from the back of the shelf each season. Catching one damp sleeve early saves the records next to it.
  • Air-conditioned rooms help, because air-conditioning also removes moisture. If only one room in the house is cooled, that is where the records belong.

Moisture absorbers kept inside a closed cabinet are often suggested for the monsoon months. If you use them, keep them from touching the records and replace them regularly, since a saturated absorber stops working.

Use the right sleeves

  • Inner sleeves. Many records come in plain paper inner sleeves, which shed fibres and can scuff the playing surface over years. Plastic-lined (poly-lined) or anti-static inner sleeves protect the disc better and do not hold damp the way paper does.
  • Outer sleeves. A clear plastic outer sleeve over the jacket protects the artwork from dust, shelf wear and humidity. It is worth it for gatefolds and for anything you would not want to replace, such as a first edition.
  • Store the disc and jacket together, but not tightly. Some collectors keep the record in its inner sleeve beside the jacket inside the outer sleeve, rather than inside the jacket, to avoid seam splits and ring wear.

Handle records by the edges

Fingerprints leave oils in the grooves that attract dust. Hold a record by its edge and the centre label only, put it back in its sleeve as soon as it has been played, and never leave it lying on the platter or on a table. Dust that settles on an uncovered record is ground into the grooves by the stylus the next time it plays. Moving or posting records

Records travel best upright in a snug box, with the discs taken out of their jackets (still in inner sleeves) to stop the edges splitting the seams. When you receive records in the post during summer or the monsoon, open the parcel indoors and let the records settle to room temperature before playing them.

A quick checklist

  1. Upright, never stacked flat.
  2. Away from sunlight, kitchens, appliances and outside walls.
  3. In the most stable room in the house — ideally one that is air-conditioned.
  4. Poly-lined inner sleeves, clear outer sleeves for records that matter.
  5. Handled by the edges, sleeved straight after playing.
  6. Checked after every monsoon.
