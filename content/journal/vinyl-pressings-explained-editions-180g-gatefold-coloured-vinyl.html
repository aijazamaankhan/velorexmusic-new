Record listings are full of shorthand: "1st edition", "180g", "gatefold", "2LP", "splatter", "picture disc", "10 inch". Each one describes something physical about the record in your hands, and some of them matter more than others. This guide explains what each term means, what it changes and what it does not — using records that are on the Velorex shelf as examples.

Original pressing, reissue, edition

A pressing is a batch of records made from the same set of metal parts at a pressing plant. When a record sells out and the label makes more, that is a repress. When a label brings an old album back — often years later, often with new artwork or new mastering — that is a reissue.

Most of the Hindi film soundtracks on vinyl today are reissues. Labels such as Saregama, Universal, Sony and T-Series have been returning classic scores to the format, which is why you can buy soundtracks from the 1970s pressed within the last few years — for example the Saregama reissues of Aandhi and Gomti Ke Kinare.

"1st edition" and "2nd edition" on a modern reissue usually mean the first and second production runs of that reissue, not the original release from decades ago. We list Rocky (1st edition) and Rocky (2nd edition) separately because they are different runs. When the difference between two runs matters to you — sleeve details, vinyl colour, insert — ask the seller what distinguishes them, because labels do not always document it.

Weight: what "180g" means

A standard 12-inch LP typically weighs somewhere around 120–140 grams. "180g" or "heavyweight" vinyl uses more material, which makes the disc stiffer and a little less prone to warping and flexing. Records such as Jannat (180g) and Dil To Pagal Hai (180g, gatefold, 3rd edition) are pressed this way.

What weight does not do on its own is guarantee better sound. How a record sounds depends mostly on the mastering and on the quality of the pressing itself. A well-made standard-weight pressing can sound better than a careless heavy one. Treat "180g" as a sign of a sturdier disc, not as a sound-quality grade.

Sleeves: gatefold, inserts and inner sleeves

  • Gatefold — a sleeve that opens like a book, giving room for photographs, liner notes or a second disc. The Dil To Pagal Hai reissue uses one.
  • Inserts — lyric sheets, posters or booklets. On a pre-owned record, check that they are all present if the listing mentions them.
  • Inner sleeve — the paper or plastic sleeve that holds the disc inside the jacket. Plain paper sheds fibres and can scuff the surface over time; plastic-lined ("poly-lined") inner sleeves are gentler.

Two discs: 2LP sets

A long soundtrack may not fit on two sides of one LP without cramming the grooves, which costs volume and clarity. Splitting it across two records keeps each side shorter. That is why long scores and compilations appear as 2LP sets — for example Taal, Devdas (1st edition), Baazigar and the ghazal collection Sajda.

Size and speed: 12-inch, 10-inch, 7-inch

Almost every album is a 12-inch record played at 33⅓ rpm. The 7-inch format, usually at 45 rpm, was historically used for singles and EPs. The 10-inch size sits in between and is less common today; Jersey is a 10-inch release. Always check the speed marked on the label before playing — a record at the wrong speed is the most common "it sounds strange" problem.

Colour: coloured, splatter, transparent and picture discs

Black is the standard colour for vinyl, but many reissues now come in colour as a collector's edition:

  • Solid coloured vinyl — one colour through the disc, like the Life In A Metro, PK and Sapne Saajan Ke coloured LPs, or the yellow first edition of Saher.
  • Splatter — flecks of a second colour through the disc, as on Gadar: Ek Prem Katha.
  • Transparent — see-through vinyl, like the transparent silver Zubeidaa.
  • Picture discs — an image sealed inside clear vinyl, like Rockstar.

Coloured vinyl is mostly about the object and its limited run. Well-made coloured pressings can sound as good as black ones. Picture discs are the exception people most often mention: because of how they are made, many listeners find them noisier than a standard pressing, so they are usually bought to display as much as to play. If sound is your priority, choose the standard black pressing where one exists.

How to read a record listing

Put together, a listing such as "Dil To Pagal Hai – Vinyl Record (Gatefold Cover / 180g / 3rd Edition)" tells you: the third production run of this reissue, a heavier disc, and a fold-out sleeve. What it does not tell you is the condition, which is a separate question for pre-owned copies — our guide to checking a used record's condition covers that.
